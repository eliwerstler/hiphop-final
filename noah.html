Thou Shalt Not Sample

Noah Bernstein · Religion & the History of Hip Hop · S26

Hip hop has always been more than music—it's a value system. What happens when those values run up against money, specifically in the studio: who gets to sample what, who owns the tradition, and what gets lost when the lawyers get involved?

Sampling as Scripture (1986–1991)

Licensed to Ill — Beastie Boys (Def Jam, 1986)

Album 1986

The debut album Rick Rubin co-produced for Def Jam encodes the Jewish-inflected, cross-cultural entrepreneurial vision at the label's founding. Its dense sample palette — drawing on Led Zeppelin, War, and others — established the formal vocabulary of licensed borrowing that would define hip hop production ethics for decades, and sparked early landmark copyright disputes.

Grand Upright Music v. Warner Bros. Records (1991)

Legal Case 1991

Judge Kevin Duffy's ruling against Biz Markie for sampling Gilbert O'Sullivan's "Alone Again" without clearance opened his opinion with "Thou shalt not steal" — invoking religious moral language inside a federal courtroom. This case became the legal watershed requiring artists to clear all samples, fundamentally reshaping the economics and creative freedom of hip hop production.

Navigating the Aftermath (2003–2012)

Shangri-La Studio — Rick Rubin's Recording Space, Malibu

Photograph

Rubin named his recording studio after the mythical paradise from Tibetan Buddhist cosmology, embedding a spiritual orientation into the physical site of production. A Jewish producer deliberately invoking Eastern religious geography reflects his eclectic, genre-crossing approach to the sacred and the secular — the same syncretic impulse that brought him to Def Jam alongside Russell Simmons in 1984.

"Bring the Noise" — Public Enemy (1987) & re-release with Anthrax (1991)

Song 1987 / 1991

Produced by the Bomb Squad under Rick Rubin's Def Jam umbrella, this track's wall-of-sound sample collage was a conscious religious-political statement — "Elvis was a hero to most / but he never meant sh-- to me." The 1991 metal collaboration exemplifies genre-bending as cultural translation, and the track's clearing difficulties later illustrated how sample costs could silence politically charged art.

Steve Stoute HRH Q&A Transcript (April 27, 2026)

Transcript 2026

Stoute's classroom discussion at Columbia's Religion and History of Hip Hop course offers first-person testimony on how financial incentives and religious values shaped artists' decisions to enter or avoid the industry. His accounts of Kirk Franklin, the Jehovah's Witness producer, and McDonald's "I'm Lovin' It" illuminate the triangulation of faith, commerce, and cultural advocacy that defines hip hop's production economy.

"Sing About Me, I'm Dying of Thirst" — Kendrick Lamar, good kid, m.A.A.d city (2012)

Song 2012

Produced by Sounwave, this track integrates explicit religious confession and mortality into a hip hop format — a direct descendant of the gospel-to-secular pipeline Stoute describes. Its sample-free production (built around live instrumentation and studio construction) reflects an artist consciously sidestepping the copyright minefield, while its theological depth affirms religion's ongoing structural role in hip hop's value system.
